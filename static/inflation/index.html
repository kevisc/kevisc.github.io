Understanding Inflation

Why official CPI systematically understates true inflation, especially for essential goods and middle-class households. Explore money supply growth (ΔM), productivity changes (ΔA), and the hidden inflation gap.

The Inflation Measurement Gap

According to official CPI, inflation averaged about 2.5% annually from 1990-2020. But if you look at what people actually buy, the picture is very different.

The Core Issue

Housing, healthcare, education, and food make up 60-70% of household spending. All rose faster than CPI. Meanwhile, the categories that DID deflate (electronics, apparel) only represent ~25% of spending.

This isn't a minor discrepancy—it's a systematic measurement problem that hides the true erosion of purchasing power for essentials.

Three Types of Inflation

1. Monetary Inflation (Money Supply Growth)

The increase in broad money supply (M2). From 1990-2020, M2 per capita grew about 5% annually—twice as fast as official CPI.

2. Consumer Price Inflation (CPI)

The "official" measure tracking a basket of goods. But the basket composition, weighting, and quality adjustments all systematically understate inflation.

3. Asset Price Inflation

Stocks, bonds, real estate going up. When money supply grows but gets concentrated at the top, you get asset inflation without consumer price inflation.

The Gap: Money supply per capita (+5%/yr) → Official CPI (+2.5%/yr) → But essentials rose +3-4%/yr or more. Where did the inflation go? Into assets held by the wealthy, and into essential goods that can't be outsourced.

How CPI Understates Inflation

Hedonic Adjustments

Example: A new car costs $40,000 vs $15,000 in 1990 (167% increase). But CPI says new cars only rose 22% because they're "better quality."

While some adjustment makes sense, this dramatically understates what you actually pay.

Substitution Bias

CPI assumes when beef gets expensive, you buy chicken instead. So it measures the "cost of survival," not the cost of the same lifestyle.

This masks declining living standards as "stable prices."

Owner's Equivalent Rent

Instead of tracking house prices, CPI tracks estimated rental equivalent. Lower interest rates reduce monthly payments even as house prices soar.

If you pay cash or want to buy, this grossly understates your cost.

Weighting Games

CPI gives housing a 33% weight, but many households spend 40-50% on housing. Tech (which DID deflate) gets overweighted relative to real spending.

The Bottom Line

Comparing apples-to-apples, the "American Dream" basket (house, kids, education, healthcare, car, quality food) has inflated closer to 3-4% annually, not 2.5%.

What this means over 30 years:

  • Official CPI: Prices doubled (2x)
  • Reality for essentials: Prices tripled (3x) or more
  • Money supply per capita: Grew 4.5x
  • Bottom 90% wealth share: Fell from 40% to 30%

ΔM (money growth) − ΔA (productivity) = net inflation

The Theory: ΔM − ΔA

For any good, the net price change ≈ Money Supply Growth (ΔM) − Productivity Growth (ΔA)

Examples

  • Smartphones: ΔM = 5%, ΔA = 20% → Prices fall 15%
  • Healthcare: ΔM = 5%, ΔA = -1% → Prices rise 6%
  • Housing: ΔM = 5%, ΔA = 1% → Prices rise 4%
Key Insight: CPI heavily weights goods with high ΔA (tech) that deflate, while underweighting services with low ΔA that inflate rapidly. This creates the measurement gap.

Why This Matters

Notice how sectors with low productivity growth (healthcare, education, housing) see the fastest price increases. These are the sectors that:

  • Can't be outsourced to cheaper labor markets
  • Can't be easily automated
  • Represent the bulk of middle-class spending
  • Are often mandatory or essential
Result: Official CPI says 2.5%, but your cost of living went up 3-4% or more if you need housing, healthcare, and education.

What About Deflation?

Yes, some categories genuinely deflated:

Electronics & Apparel

Smartphones, TVs, clothing all got cheaper due to massive productivity gains and offshoring to China/Southeast Asia.

Problem: These are only 25% of household spending

Why the Gap Exists

Essentials (housing, healthcare, education, food) = 70% of spending, inflated 3-4%/yr

Discretionary (electronics, apparel) = 25% of spending, deflated

CPI averages these and says 2.5%

The iPad Argument: In 2011, NY Fed Chair Dudley told inflation-concerned Queens residents that an iPad 2 came out at the same price as iPad 1 but was better, so on a quality-adjusted basis, prices fell. Someone yelled back: "You can't eat an iPad!"

Who Benefits, Who Gets Squeezed?

Inflation's impact depends heavily on your balance sheet and income source.

Top 10% (Winners)

  • Debt/Equity ratio: ~2% (almost all assets, minimal debt)
  • Asset ownership: 70% of stocks, bonds, real estate
  • Income source: Capital gains, dividends (grew with money supply)
  • Spending: Small % on essentials, large % on discretionary

Why They Win

Their assets inflate with money supply growth. Essential goods are a small % of their budget. They benefit from cheap debt and rising asset prices.

Bottom 50% (Losers)

  • Debt/Equity ratio: ~200% (more debt than equity)
  • Asset ownership: 2% of total household wealth
  • Income source: Wages (flat in real terms for 40 years)
  • Spending: 70%+ on essentials (housing, food, healthcare)

Why They Lose

Wages barely keep up with CPI. But actual costs (essentials) rose faster. They have little asset exposure to benefit from money supply growth. Need more education/debt just to earn median wage.

The Productivity Paradox

What Happened

From 1990-2020:

  • Worker productivity rose ~75%
  • Median wages rose ~25% (barely keeping pace with essentials inflation)
  • Corporate profits as % of GDP rose dramatically
  • Automation and offshoring displaced labor

The Result

Workers became more productive but didn't capture those gains. Instead, productivity gains went to:

  • Capital owners (shareholders)
  • Consumers (via lower prices for manufactured goods)
  • NOT to labor (wages stagnated)
So the working class got cheaper iPhones and t-shirts, but housing, healthcare, and education ate up any savings—and then some.

Why Policymakers Allow This

Benefits of Underreporting

  • Social Security COLAs are lower (saves government money)
  • Can claim "stable prices" while running loose monetary policy
  • Inflates away government debt quietly
  • Boosts asset prices (makes wealthy happy)

Costs of Honest Reporting

  • Would show wages haven't kept pace with reality
  • Would force higher Social Security payments
  • Would reveal actual decline in living standards
  • Would limit ability to run loose monetary policy
The Trade-Off: Accurate inflation measurement vs political/fiscal convenience. Current system chose convenience.
